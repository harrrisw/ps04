What’s On My Mind: The Road to MAICON 2026
This week, I’m packing up to head to Cleveland for MAICON, the Marketing AI Conference, now in year 7. I have a special fondness for this event; long before generative AI, Paul Roetzer and team had built this community of AI-forward marketers and technologists to talk about the future of marketing with AI. I have the privilege of being the very first MAICON keynote, back in 2019, and that talk is still available though very, very outdated now.
It’s funny because looking back, all the classical AI things I talked about – forecasting, predictive analytics, natural language processing – are hot once again because generative AI gives non-experts access to these methods. You can talk to Claude Code or ChatGPT Codex or what have you and have it create any of those methodologies as software that you can run without having to pay anything for it.
But today, let’s talk about the road to MAICON and what I’m doing with my various tools to get ready for the event.
Part 1: Before I Leave
MAICON is also unusual for me because I like to drive there. it’s about a 600 mile / 1000 km drive, a nice straight shot on I-90 from Boston to Cleveland with plenty of stops and scenery along the way. One year, I had a friend from the UK visiting and we made a stop at Niagara Falls. Without traffic or stops, it’s about 9.5 hours of driving, easily doable within a day. Most years, it’s about 11 hours with stops for things like meals, tourist attractions, etc.
This year, I’ve built a few pieces of support for me along the way so I can think and work a bit as I drive. The first is what I call slopcasts. These are AI-generated podcasts, read aloud by a TTS (text to speech) system based on academic papers I’ve been meaning to read but haven’t gotten around to. Google’s Gemini Notebooks (formerly NotebookLM) do this very capably, but with a fair number of restrictions and a methodology that isn’t terribly flexible. You also can’t do any customization of the voices or of their artificially created speech disfluencies, which can be tough to listen to after a while.
For myself, I’ve built a skill for my Hermes Agent that assembles these for me using lots of deterministic tools. I’ll tell my agent what papers I want it to retrieve, like the gold medal papers from an academic conference, then ingest those papers, draft a speaking script from the paper that summarizes the key points in my preferred dialect, and feeds the speaking script to my TTS, Breeze2-TTS, a local TTS that runs on my Mac and generates good quality spoken word audio for free. Once that’s done, it puts them in a place any audio player can reach, like a Dropbox folder, and then I can listen to the papers summarized my way.
What makes this work for me is the integration with my agent. I build the skill in a frontier model like Claude Code, then load the skill onto the agent so it doesn’t have to try to build it itself. Agents like Hermes, when plugged into free, local models like Qwen3.6 35B are capable of doing, but not always great at thinking or coding. If I build the skills first in the best models, then the local models only have to execute the task, not build it themselves.
And because it’s under my control, I can tune the speaking script exactly the way I want without having to fight the built-in system prompts like in Gemini Notebook.
The second setup within my agent is a connection to the MAICON conference app. The SmarterX team very sensibly created a conference app this year that’s a web app, which means any agent that can use a browser can access the app and interact on our behalf. My agent has instructions to check the app once an hour and notify me if there’s anything worth me responding to, and I message it right from Discord to do that.
As I’m out and about for the conference, I’ll be able to have the agent keep looking on my behalf, so I don’t have to constantly be on my phone. Discord can message me when there’s something I need to pay attention to.
My third and final skill I built just for the road to MAICON is a heavily revised version of Andrej Karpathy’s LLM-wiki skill. LLM-wiki, as he designed it, is a simple markdown-based wiki that an agent uses as a journal of sorts. That’s a fine start, but I want it to do much more. I’ve reconfigured the stock LLM-wiki skill built into Hermes Agent into a true multimedia journaling app.
From Discord or the communications app of my choice, I can load video, audio, or images to the Hermes channel. Behind the scenes, it fires some Python scripts and local AI to convert and transcribe video and audio, storing that in the wiki. For images, it uses Qwen3.6’s vision capabilities to digest photos and images. Then inside the wiki itself, I’ve installed the SilverBullet server along with QMD (query markdown) that provides lexical and semantic search.
Other things I’ve bought or prepped for this trip include a portable, battery-powered espresso maker (no more conference/hotel coffee!), plenty of power banks and power strips, AC adapters for the car to power a heating pad for my back (yay being in your 50s), and my usual bag of AV tricks. On site, Katie Robbert and I will be doing a live version of the Trust Insights podcast, In-Ear Insights, so I’ve got my own camera and mics for that as backups for the main show ones.
I’m also filling my external hard drive with piles of local models. I’ve promised a few friends like Andy Crestodina, Becca Bultsma, Chad Treadway, and Angie Bailey that we’ll take some time to do an informal mini-workshop on setting up an agent like Hermes Agent that can have the same functionality as an agent like Meta Muse, but much safer and more private. Some of the models I’m packing include Qwen3 Embedding, EmbeddingGemma2, Qwen3.6 35B-A3B, Gemma 4 26B-A4B, Gemma 4 E4B, and others – the handful of models I know work well with agents and can run on robust hardware.
Part 2: Hitting the Road
11 hours on the road is a long time, but it’s a good time, too. As I said, I have my slopcasts, but I’m much more interested in creating content as I drive – but doing it safely. The LLM-wiki skill takes in things like voice memos and voice recordings, so my plan is to generate voice memos as I drive, then load them into my agent either at rest stops, or when I get to Cleveland. The agent will then transcribe, summarize, and map them with the wiki skill. And because I’m running this entirely on my laptop, I don’t need internet or huge cloud compute to get these memos transcribed into text.
The reason an application like QMD is so important is because it contains both lexical and semantic search – exact matches on words and phrases as well as finding topics and connections. When I have an AI agent try to draw connections among my different voice memos, that type of searching, called reciprocal rank fusion, will let the agent understand how different recordings and topics relate to each other.
What this means for me is that I can foam at the mouth and just talk for up to 11 hours straight about my thoughts on nearly anything, have the machine process that, then create a conceptual map. From that map and that corpus of my real words, if I want to generate new, polished content like books, webinars, etc., I can use the knowledge graph that tools like QMD and graphify can create, feed the graph to an AI agent, and later have it pull out my exact words from the transcripts to assemble them into new content.
I’ll give you a prompt I find superbly helpful with AI transcription, something I haven’t seen anywhere else. One of the challenges I’ve had with transcript cleanup is that very often, AI rewrites my words into things I’ve never said and worse, makes it sound like slop. The way around this is to be very clear what AI is allowed to do with a transcript:
Audit the following transcript for speech disfluencies, grammar, and clarity. Make a list of the minimum effective edits needed to remove speech disfluencies, fix grammar, and improve clarity. You are permitted to recommend changes in sentence and paragraph order and add paragraph breaks to improve clarity. Word-level deletion is permitted, but you may not recommend adding more than 5% new words (measure with python def count_words(text): return len(text.split())). The goal is to preserve the speaker’s voice, style, and diction, so identify the smallest effective edits needed to do so. Return the list of suggested edits and explanations in Markdown format for user approval.
There’s a much bigger, better, more robust process I share in my AI for Writers Course, but this is an excellent lightweight version that preserves my words at the sentence level.
I’ve built this prompt into a skill for my agent, so it can do lightweight cleanup as it adds my thoughts to the wiki for later use.
I’ve also downloaded the entire MAICON conference agenda in Markdown format into my agent, so in addition to using the app, I can have it read up on various sessions. As I drive, one of the exercises I’ll be doing is taking each session description, one by one, and giving my own thoughts about the session and topic as though I were delivering the session. How would I approach the topic? What key points do I think would be critical in the session? It’s sort of like an alternate MAICON, if I were to do the entire conference myself.
I do this because I want to stretch my own knowledge and thinking. Every speaker has their perspective on their topic, but I wonder how I’d handle the same topic. This will give me lots of raw material for new works in a clean-room version. It’s important that I do this on the way to MAICON before I’ve seen their sessions, so that my words are mine alone, not influenced by them or their content.
And if there’s a session topic where I’m struggling to come up with anything because I don’t know the topic well, that’s a pretty solid indicator I need to attend that session because there’s a gap in my knowledge.
Part 3: On-Site Plans
Conferences can be crazy places, filled with barely organized chaos and scheduling conflicts galore. The big challenge for me will be keeping track of all the things, especially outside the official schedule, that I’m supposed to be doing – dinner, coffee, breakfast, meetups, etc. I connected my Hermes Agent to my Google Calendar using the Google Workspace CLI (command line interface, aka the terminal or command prompt). This is a very token-efficient way for an agent to talk to a third party service AND it keeps the authentication for the service separate from the agent.
If the agent were to try going rogue, I could deauthorize the command line tool itself on my agent’s server and then no matter its best efforts, it wouldn’t be able to access Google services because it wouldn’t have any way to recover passwords or authorization tokens. This is one of those little secrets for making agents work well. The agent should be given tools that it doesn’t have control over, in terms of access and authentication. That way you can turn off the tool, and the agent loses access immediately in a much more reliable way. It’s like an emergency stop button where you can just hit stop and the agent no longer can perform some functions.
By having my calendar connected to my agent, I can check my Google Calendar app when someone says, “Oh hey Chris, we’re doing this thing at this time” and if I’m free and it’s something I want to do, I can have my agent block that time for me, store details, etc. And I can do something else.
That’s the secret to these agents, what makes them powerful and effective. I am perfectly capable of checking and setting things on my calendar by myself. I am a minimally functional adult. But it’s much less effort on my part to simply dictate to my agent, like a personal assistant, and have it manage the details with the skills I built for it. Because I built the skills, I know what it’s supposed to do (and more important, it has its own tests to measure whether it did the thing) so I can dash off a thought and immediately go back to do something else (like paying attention to the person in front of me) and let it work. Is it slower than me? Yes. But the point is to delegate and let it do the thing without me.
During the show, I’ll also use my LLM-wiki skill to jot down notes and thoughts as I have them in various sessions. In the past, I’ve written down notes and then promptly forgotten where I put them, or couldn’t remember the context around them. Since my agent already has the entire show downloaded in its memory, it will be able to append that context as I make notes, entering it in the journal in the wiki. When I go to reconstruct it later, I’ll have much more context to work with.
Part 4: The Road Home
After the show ends on Thursday, I drive back on Friday. The Friday drive is all about processing, all about thinking aloud about the people I met, the topics I talked about, the insights I gathered, and the things I want to build. One of the things about a conference like MAICON is that I see ideas that I want to bring to life myself my way. And so I’ll have a running catalog of things that I want to build at some point when I’m no longer at the conference.
This will be 11 more hours of me talking aloud, but also reviewing, having the agent recite things, doing supplementary research on the go from things I heard about. For example, often someone will have a quote or a citation from a study, and I’ll make note of that. During their talk, I can have the agent go retrieve the paper, feed it to the slopcast skill, and then on the drive home listen to the digest of the paper.
That way, I get the full context of what the speaker was saying. For example, my friend Nancy Harhut cites over a dozen different papers in her excellent talks on behavioral psychology. In the past, I’ve made note of them but haven’t gone to go grab all of the papers, just the ones that I remembered. Now, I can queue them up for my agent to go retrieve and digest, turning them into slopcasts for me to listen to on the drive back for added context.
After all is said and done, I’ll have a large Markdown wiki, and I’ll want to make use of it. Tools like SilverBullet can run right on my agent, providing me with a private website to review the entire wiki, like my own personal website of the conference including the days leading up to it and afterward.
Part 5: Wrapping Up
The big meta takeaway I want you to grab from this week’s newsletter is that agentic AI systems work best today when you structure them as orchestrators, as executors of tasks – but you build the skills beforehand with your best AI tools.
Think of skills like apps. A phone or computer with no apps wouldn’t be a terribly useful device, no matter how powerful the hardware or operating system. The difference is that today, AI coding tools can build us any app we can think of, so as you prepare for conferences, events, big meetings, or anything else where you’d like to have some kind of virtual assistant handy, think through the specific skills you’d like that assistant to have. That’s what you need to build with AI before you start using your agent.
Once you do that, the sky’s the limit and the open road awaits you.
