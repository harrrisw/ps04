## Narration

**Narration:**
AI research tools are changing in a way that matters to small businesses: they’re moving from “write me something” toward “read this source material and show your work.” That shift is the main story here, not bigger promises about automation. [claim:01-c1]

For a small team, that matters because the real bottleneck is usually not drafting a summary. It’s finding the right files, keeping the evidence visible, and making sure the result can be checked later. Crayon’s SharePoint announcement and its Insights API both point in that direction: tools are being built to pull from internal repositories and return source-linked outputs. [claim:01-c1] [claim:01-c3]

Nielsen Norman Group’s guidance reinforces the same principle from a research-methods angle. Their empathy-mapping article says every note should trace back to a specific research artifact, and their Siri article argues that an assistant should answer from information already available to it rather than forcing users to re-enter it. [claim:01-c2] [claim:01-c5]

So the practical change is this: for customer, competitor, and market research, AI is becoming more useful as a retrieval-and-checking layer before synthesis. That means asking it to gather context, identify what’s missing, and point back to the source material before you decide what the story is. [claim:01-c4] [claim:01-c5]

A good way to picture it is a simple workflow: **source files → AI retrieval → visible citations or links → human review → decision-ready summary**. This is an illustrative diagram, but it matches the evidence-backed pattern in the sources. The common thread is traceability, not magic. [claim:01-c1] [claim:01-c4]

For small-business research teams, that opens a narrow but real pilot opportunity. Use AI where it can help with desk research, competitor monitoring, and synthesis of interviews or surveys — especially when the task is to pull in context from approved files or connected apps and then surface contradictions or gaps. [claim:01-c2] [claim:01-c4]

The caution is just as important. These sources are mostly vendor announcements and practitioner guidance, not controlled field trials. Crayon’s statements are attributed product claims; they do not prove market-wide performance or ROI. And the tutorial examples show possible workflows, not measured speedups or accuracy against a baseline. [claim:01-c1]

So the right question is not, “Has AI research replaced the researcher?” It hasn’t. The better question is, “Can this tool reduce search and organization time while preserving enough evidence traceability to support a decision?” That is the threshold a small business can test without overcommitting. [claim:01-c4]

My bottom line: the meaningful novelty is not just faster text generation. It’s better retrieval, better source linkage, and clearer visibility into what the model could not see. If a tool can do that inside a narrow workflow, it may be worth a pilot. If it can’t show its sources or limits, the claim still needs validation. [claim:01-c4] [claim:01-c5]
