## Scene 1

If you run research for a small business, AI offers an appealing possibility: less time sorting customer comments, organizing interviews, or checking a research memo. But what makes the output reliable enough to guide a decision?

Across three sources, the strongest case is for practices that keep evidence inspectable. These sources do not establish comparative accuracy, lower total cost, or reliable time savings. The question is whether automation leaves you with useful findings—and enough time to check them.

## Scene 2

Christopher Penn describes lightweight models that classify text into categories, scores, or yes-or-no answers. For customer feedback, that could mean assigning comments to categories you define.

His key constraint: you supply the data and context. The model does not gather that foundation for you.

Penn reports better accuracy after tuning his own model on his own data. That is an attributed personal account, without a benchmark, sample, or measured cost comparison. It does not establish that small models generally replace frontier models. Penn also promotes consulting and training, which matters when weighing his claims.

## Scene 3

Nielsen Norman Group provides a useful rule for empathy maps: every note should connect to a specific research artifact, such as a transcript passage, recording moment, or observation. Without that connection, label the note as an assumption.

That distinction matters when AI helps organize interviews or open-ended survey answers. A plausible summary can still outrun the underlying evidence. Keep the connection between the proposed theme and the material supporting it.

This is methodological guidance. It is not evidence that AI improves synthesis, and generated customer statements cannot substitute for observed customer evidence.

## Scene 4

Marketing AI Institute describes using agents to flag claims, suggest corrections, and supply source links for human review. It also explicitly warns that multiple agents can agree on the same wrong answer.

So a second AI agreeing with the first is not a substitute for inspecting the source. A proposed correction still needs checking.

The Institute reports time savings, but supplies no controlled evaluation of this workflow. It also promotes commercial education. Treat the account as a workflow to consider, with benefits that remain unmeasured for your business.

## Scene 5

Together, these sources support an editorial inference: for interview or survey synthesis, checking a finding against its original research artifact is a stronger acceptance rule than relying on agreement between agents. This is our synthesis, not a measured comparison of tools.

For a small pilot, choose one bounded task. Define the categories or research question, retain source references, and inspect the proposed findings before using them.

Track preparation and review effort alongside processing time. Watch for unsupported conclusions and missed exceptions. This is a proposed evaluation approach, not a tested result. The counterargument deserves attention: checking the work consumes scarce time too. These sources cannot tell us whether the complete workflow saves time for a small firm.

## Scene 6

Transfer to your business remains uncertain. Document volume, specialist expertise, and staffing may differ substantially. Geographic applicability is unspecified, and non-English performance is untested. Direct small-business users, unsuccessful deployments, and the customers whose data gets analyzed are missing perspectives. Nothing here establishes that disclosing customer data to a tool is harmless.

The practical takeaway: pilot a specific task where you can inspect the evidence, then judge the complete effort. Traceability gives you something concrete to check; broad performance promises still need validation.
