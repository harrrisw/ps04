## Scene 1

If you run research for a small business, the useful AI question is practical: can it help you answer one business question, with evidence you can check, at an effort you can afford?

This brief proposes a bounded customer-synthesis pilot. Start with research material you already hold, name the person responsible for interpretation, and compare the full workload with your current manual process. This is an editorial proposal, not a tested intervention or a promise of savings.

## Scene 2

Nielsen Norman Group makes a useful distinction: asking AI to organize research you have is different from asking it to generate evidence you do not possess.

For a pilot, that means working from retained interview notes, survey responses, or support tickets. Every proposed finding should point back to its source. If you cannot locate that support, label the finding as an assumption.

A polished summary is not enough. Check whether its themes preserve what participants actually said, including contradictions and outliers. Synthetic customer statements cannot stand in for observed customer evidence.

## Scene 3

What counts as success? In Greenbook’s interview, Wex practitioner Nadine van Rooyen argues that an insight’s success should not depend solely on whether its recommendation gets implemented.

That gives a small-business pilot a useful question: did the checked findings improve understanding of the decision, even if the business chose to wait?

But Wex is a global enterprise with specialist roles. This practitioner account does not establish small-business return on investment, staffing requirements, or AI effectiveness.

## Scene 4

Context also takes work. In Insight Platforms’ interview, South African researcher Mpho Mpofu calls for budgets to accommodate language and cultural context.

For your pilot, identify who can check local meaning, and include that effort in the comparison. Do not assume an English summary captures every nuance.

Mpofu’s perspective concerns varied African markets. It should not become a single regional customer profile. These English-language interviews also do not directly represent non-English customers or small-business AI deployments.

## Scene 5

Together, these sources support our inference: assess traceability and contextual fit before treating AI outputs as decision evidence. Producing a recommendation, or getting it implemented, is insufficient by itself.

Keep the pilot narrow. Record preparation, AI-assisted organization, human checking, corrections, and later maintenance. Compare those tasks with the current manual process, using the same question and material. Record actual costs rather than assuming savings.

Name an interpretation owner. Preserve unresolved questions. Customer-data handling and consent need separate evidence before disclosure to a tool.

Expand only if the comparison supports it. The sources reviewed here do not demonstrate that this pilot design works.

## Scene 6

Watch for independent small-business evaluations that disclose manual baselines, total costs, source coverage, multilingual checks, and failures. Those results are missing here, as are customers’ views on data use and documented unsuccessful small-business deployments.

Read these perspectives with their incentives in mind: Mpofu has book, research-business, and training interests; Greenbook promotes conferences; Insight Platforms operates a directory and conferences.

The next step is a small, reviewable comparison. Keep the evidence, count the checking, and judge whether the work helps the decision.
