## Scene 1

Can AI make competitor research faster while leaving you with evidence you can actually check? For a small business, that is the useful question behind the latest connected research tools. Getting material into an AI workflow may become easier. But access is only one part of dependable research. Three recent sources point toward a practical response: try a bounded pilot, and evaluate retrieval, source inspection, maintenance, and human control together.

## Scene 2

Crayon says its announced Insights API makes competitor insights available to AI tools or developers. Alongside details such as titles, summaries, dates, and competitor tags, it says users can request underlying text and source links. That matters because a researcher could inspect the material behind a summary.

But this is a commercial capability claim from the company selling it. The evidence reviewed here does not establish accuracy, pricing, eligibility, geographic coverage, or how much implementation work a small business would face. Treat the announcement as a capability to investigate, with affordability and usefulness still open.

## Scene 3

Nielsen Norman Group adds a constraint. In its study of expert Claude users, participants reportedly spent more effort engineering context libraries than crafting prompts. Files became stale or went missing. One participant’s task-list connection repeatedly expired and needed manual reconnection.

For research, that raises a practical question: will the right background material still be available and current next week? The supplied study text gives neither sample size nor geography. These expert-user experiences identify problems worth testing; they do not tell us how often small businesses will encounter them, or how much time maintenance will take.

## Scene 4

A different setting supplies another caution. TechCrunch reports that Anthropic said it turned off live internet access for its internal evaluations until it could confidently monitor and control its agents.

That establishes a reported company disclosure. It is not independent verification of the incidents, and it gives us no failure rate for deployed research products. Frontier-lab evaluations also differ substantially from a small firm’s research work. Still, the disclosure makes human oversight a relevant question when considering connected agents. These sources address different settings; together, they are not a comparative tool test.

## Scene 5

Our inference is to test the whole workflow over repeated runs. Choose one competitor question and a limited set of public sources. Check whether summaries match the underlying passages, whether links remain usable, and whether dates and competitor labels are correct. Record verification time and connection repairs alongside any time saved. Establish actual access costs and setup effort before expanding.

A source link helps you investigate an answer. It does not establish that the workflow stays usable. Decide in advance what evidence would justify continuing, and what errors or maintenance burden would make you stop. Keep the final research judgment with a person. This is a proposed pilot, not a reported result.

## Scene 6

The evidence leaves important gaps. We lack direct accounts from small-business users, unsuccessful deployments, and customers whose data is analyzed. These English-language sources do not establish performance in non-English markets. They also support no conclusion about customer-data handling or consent. Customer-feedback, interview, and survey workflows therefore need separate assessment.

The opportunity is easier access to research material. Whether that becomes affordable, dependable research depends on what your pilot reveals about checking sources, maintaining connections, and retaining control.
